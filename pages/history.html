The Building Hiroshima Chose to Keep

Hiroshima preserved the ruins of an atomic bomb — and turned them into a lesson about peace for the next generation.

| Peace Education | | 1 min |

In Hiroshima, schoolchildren are taken to the remains of a building that survived the atomic bomb.
The Atomic Bomb Dome still stands beside the peaceful Hiroshima Peace Memorial Park, deliberately preserved as a reminder of what happened in 1945. But the lesson isn't simply about destruction.
Hiroshima's peace education teaches children about the bombing, the value of human life, respect for others, and the importance of building a peaceful world.
Instead of allowing the memory of tragedy to become only anger, the city uses it to ask a different question: How do we make sure this never happens again?

02

The Dancing Plague of 1518

In 1518, hundreds of people in Strasbourg suddenly started dancing. Some couldn't stop for days.

| France | Dancing Mystery | | 2 min |

In the summer of 1518, something bizarre happened in Strasbourg.
A woman began dancing in the street. Then another person joined. Then another.
Within days, dozens of people were dancing uncontrollably. The outbreak eventually involved hundreds. Some reportedly danced for days, barely stopping to eat or sleep. Historical records even suggest that authorities believed the best solution was to let people dance it out, opening venues and hiring musicians.
It didn't work.
The dancing eventually stopped, but historians still debate what caused it. One explanation is mass psychogenic illness — where extreme stress and social influence can produce real physical symptoms in groups of people.
Whatever happened, the Dancing Plague remains one of history's strangest mysteries.

03

When Tigers Smoked

Korean folktales often begin with the phrase: “Back when tigers smoked.”

| Korea | Folklore | | 2 min |

Korean folktales have a traditional opening:
“호랑이 담배 피우던 시절에”
horangi dambae piudeon shijeore
roughly, “back when tigers smoked.”
It means “a very long time ago”, similar to saying “once upon a time.”
The phrase comes from a time when tobacco had already become established in Korea, and tigers were a common figure in Korean folklore. Tigers appeared frequently in traditional stories and were sometimes portrayed as frightening, foolish, or surprisingly human-like characters.
So when a Korean story begins with “back when tigers smoked,” it's simply an old way of saying: “This happened a very long time ago.”
