The Self-Awareness Paradox

What happens when you become so aware of yourself that you stop knowing which version of you is real?

| Self-Awareness | | 3 min |

Fernando Pessoa spent much of his life writing under different names — except they weren't simply pen names. He gave them different personalities, writing styles, biographies and worldviews.
And most of the literary world barely knew him during his lifetime. His fame grew dramatically after his death.
Pessoa's writing often blurred the boundary between reality, imagination and identity. If we are constantly observing our own thoughts, feelings and behaviour, how do we know which version of ourselves is the "real" one?
That's the strange part of self-awareness.
Knowing yourself better doesn't necessarily make you feel more certain about who you are. Sometimes, it gives you more versions of yourself to question.

02

The Phantom Vibration

You feel your phone vibrate in your pocket but when you check it...it's nothing.

| Perception | | 1 min |

You feel your phone vibrate. You reach into your pocket. Nothing.
This is known as phantom vibration syndrome. The sensation that your phone is vibrating when it isn't.
It's thought to happen because your brain becomes highly attentive to familiar signals. Tiny movements, muscle twitches or pressure against your skin can be interpreted as the vibration you're expecting.
The strange part is that your brain doesn't need an actual phone notification to create the sensation because the expectation is enough.
Your brain interprets a real but ambiguous physical sensation like clothing moving, muscle twitches, or pressure on the skin as a phone vibration.

03

The Anxiety of Possibility

Kierkegaard thought anxiety wasn't caused by something being wrong. It came from realising that you are free to choose.

| Philosophy | | 2 min |

In The Concept of Anxiety (1844), Danish philosopher Søren Kierkegaard described anxiety as something different from fear.
Fear has an object. You are afraid of something: a dog, an exam, a dangerous situation. Anxiety can happen when there is no specific thing to fear.
Kierkegaard connected this to possibility and freedom.
Imagine standing at the edge of a tall building. You might be afraid of falling, but you can also experience something stranger: the awareness that you could jump. The possibility itself creates anxiety.
For Kierkegaard, this wasn't necessarily a problem to eliminate. Anxiety is partly a consequence of being free to choose between different possibilities.
You can study or not study.
Move somewhere new or stay.
Say something or stay silent.
There are possibilities everywhere, and you are responsible for choosing between them. He described anxiety as a kind of “dizziness of freedom.”
So anxiety, in his view, isn't simply about something bad that might happen. It can come from realising that you could do something.
