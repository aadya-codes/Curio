01

The Monty Hall Problem

Three doors. One car. Two goats. Your odds are probably not what you think.

| Statistics | | 3 min |

You're on a game show with three closed doors.
Behind one door is a car.
Behind the other two are goats.
You choose Door 1.
The host, who knows where the car is, opens another door—say Door 3—revealing a goat.
Two doors remain:
Door 1 (your original choice)
Door 2
The host asks:
Would you like to switch?
Before reading further, decide:
Stay? Switch? Doesn't matter?

You should always switch. It feels like the odds become 50–50 once a goat is revealed, but they don't.
Your first choice had only a 1 in 3 chance of being correct.
That means there was a 2 in 3 chance the car was behind one of the other two doors.
When the host opens a door, they aren't choosing randomly—they deliberately reveal a goat.
So the entire 2/3 probability transfers to the one unopened door.
Stay: 1/3 chance of winning.
Switch: 2/3 chance of winning.

02

The Green-Eyed Islanders

Everyone can see everyone else's eye colour but nobody knows their own. Then a stranger says one sentence that changes everything.

| Logic | | 4 min |

You arrive on an island with 100 inhabitants.
Every person has either green or brown eyes.
They can see everyone else's eye colour, but nobody can see their own. They never discuss eye colour with each other.
Anyone who discovers that they have green eyes must leave the island at midnight.
One day, a visitor announces: “At least one person on this island has green eyes.”
You know that exactly 100 people have green eyes.
What happens?

Let's start with just one green-eyed person.
They look around and see nobody else with green eyes. After hearing the visitor's statement, they think: “If I don't have green eyes, then nobody here has green eyes. But the visitor said at least one person does. So it must be me.”
They leave that night.
Now imagine there are two green-eyed people.
Each sees one other person with green eyes. Each thinks: “If I don't have green eyes, that person is the only green-eyed person here. They should realise it and leave tonight.”
But nobody leaves. So on the second night, they both realise: “I must have green eyes too.”
They leave together.
So the pattern continues.
With 100 green-eyed people, all 100 leave on the 100th night.

04

The Two Ropes

Two ropes each take exactly an hour to burn, but neither burns at a consistent speed. Can you measure 45 minutes?

| Logic | | 2 min |

You have two ropes. Each rope takes exactly 60 minutes to burn from one end to the other.
But there's a catch: They don't burn at a consistent rate.
One half of a rope might burn in 5 minutes, while the other half takes 55 minutes.
You have a lighter, but no clock.
How can you measure exactly 45 minutes?

Light both ends of Rope A and one end of Rope B at the same time.
Because Rope A is burning from both ends, it will take exactly 30 minutes to burn completely. The uneven burning rate doesn't matter. Both flames are consuming the rope simultaneously, so the total burning time is halved.
When Rope A finishes, exactly 30 minutes have passed.
Now light the other end of Rope B.
Rope B has already been burning for 30 minutes from one end. We don't know how much rope remains, because its burning rate is uneven. But we do know that whatever remains would have taken 30 minutes to burn from one end.
Now it is burning from both ends, so it takes exactly 15 more minutes.
30 + 15 = 45 minutes.
