Some doors are easier to open than to close.

01

The Taos Hum

For decades, some people in a small New Mexico town have heard a low, distant hum that others can't hear. Scientists still haven't pinned it down.

| Mystery | Unexplained | | 3 min |

In the early 1990s, residents of Taos, New Mexico began reporting something strange. A persistent, low-pitched humming sound. And not everyone can hear it.
The sound has been described as a distant diesel engine, a vibrating bass note, or something like a refrigerator running somewhere far away.
Researchers investigated the reports and took measurements around Taos. They found that some people genuinely reported hearing a persistent noise, but they couldn't identify one external source responsible for all of it. Possible explanations have included industrial machinery, electrical equipment, distant traffic, unusual atmospheric conditions and even tinnitus or other forms of internal sound perception.
But none neatly explains every report.
Even stranger, the people who hear it aren't necessarily imagining a sound in the ordinary sense; some report physical effects such as headaches, sleep disruption or a sensation of vibration.
So is there actually a mysterious sound coming from somewhere around Taos?
Or is the mystery happening inside the people hearing it?
After more than three decades, there still isn't one definitive answer.

02

Cicada 3301

An anonymous internet account posted a puzzle in 2012 in search of 'highly intelligent individuals'.

| Mystery | Internet | | 4 min |

In January 2012, an anonymous account posted a strange image online. It contained a simple message:
“We are looking for highly intelligent individuals.”
A hidden message was embedded inside the image. Solving it led to another clue, which led to another, and another. Soon, people were using cryptography, steganography, programming, mathematics and obscure literary references to work their way through the puzzle.
But Cicada 3301 wasn't confined to the internet. Some clues eventually produced GPS coordinates. Solvers followed them to physical locations around the world like Paris, Warsawa, Miami, Erskineville, and Fayetteville, where they found posters bearing QR codes.
New puzzles appeared in 2013 and 2014, becoming increasingly complex. Some people who progressed far enough reportedly received private instructions. But nobody knew who was behind it.
Cicada 3301 never publicly identified its creators. Theories have ranged from intelligence agencies and cybersecurity organisations to recruitment campaigns and extremely elaborate games. The puzzles themselves were real. The people solving them were real. The physical posters were real.
But who created Cicada 3301 — and what they ultimately wanted from the people who solved it — remains unknown. And perhaps the internet still has people trying to solve the remaining pieces.

03

Zero-Knowledge Proofs

How do you prove you know a secret without ever revealing the secret?

| Cryptography | | 3 min |

Imagine someone claims they know the password to a vault. You don't trust them. But instead of asking them to tell you the password, you ask them to prove they know it.
They disappear behind a wall. A few seconds later, they come back out. You still don't know the password. And somehow, you've become more confident that they know it.
Now imagine doing this with something much stranger:
You have performed a calculation involving a million pieces of data. You don't show anyone the data. You don't show them your calculation. You don't even make them repeat it. And yet, you can give them a tiny mathematical proof that lets them verify: “Yes. The calculation was done correctly.”
This isn't a thought experiment. It's the idea behind zero-knowledge proofs — cryptographic techniques that allow someone to prove that a statement is true without revealing the information behind it.
Modern systems can use variants such as zk-SNARKs to create proofs that are incredibly small compared with the computations they represent. So a verifier doesn't necessarily need to trust you. They can trust the proof.

04

Wabi-Sabi

A Japanese aesthetic that finds beauty in imperfection, impermanence and simplicity.

| Japan | Aesthetics | | 2 min |

Wabi-sabi is a Japanese aesthetic associated with appreciating things that are imperfect, impermanent and incomplete.
A handmade bowl with an uneven shape can be valued for its irregularity. An old wooden object can be appreciated for signs of age and use rather than being restored to look new.
The idea is closely connected with Japanese Buddhist traditions and became particularly associated with tea culture, where simple and imperfect objects were valued over elaborate ones.
This can also be seen in kintsugi, the practice of repairing broken ceramics with lacquer and powdered metal. The cracks are not necessarily hidden; the repaired object retains evidence of its history.
Wabi-sabi is often translated into the simple idea of “beauty in imperfection,” although that doesn't capture its full historical meaning. It is also associated with the temporary nature of things: objects age, materials decay, and nothing remains unchanged.
Rather than treating these qualities as flaws, wabi-sabi treats them as part of the object's character.
